Both rows have the same expected gradient ∇J — subtracting a constant from the reward never changes where the policy is heading on average. What changes is the spread of the individual updates. With b = 0 a reward of 0 produces no update at all and a reward of 1 produces a large one; with b ≈ mean reward every sample carries information ("better or worse than usual?").

Two things to notice, both measured by the comparison table. (1) The median number of steps barely changes (with the default arms and α = 0.1, roughly 420 either way): the expected gradient is identical, and in this easy bandit the average drift dominates. (2) The tail changes a lot: raise α to 0.3–0.5, or use the "hard bandit" preset (0.9 vs 0.85), and the no-baseline runs that lock onto the wrong arm and never recover become common (in our measurements about 1 in 3 at α = 0.5 on the hard bandit, versus fewer than 1 in 10 with the running mean). Why: with b = 0 a reward of 0 on the best arm teaches nothing, while a lucky r = 1 on a mediocre arm gives it a full-size push; a few such pushes early on and the softmax has already committed. The running mean turns r = 0 into a real negative signal. The exact-variance table shows the same fact without sampling. LLM RL has exactly this shape — thousands of prompts, a few samples each, a small learning rate — which is why GRPO (Chapter 19) uses the group mean as its baseline.
